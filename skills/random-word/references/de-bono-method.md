# The random-word method: summary of the book

Source: Edward de Bono, *Lateral Thinking: A Textbook of Creativity* (1970). Chapter 18, "The random stimulus", complemented by chapter 20, "A new word: PO". This is a paraphrased summary, not a quotation. (The copy read was a Spanish edition, so chapter titles are translated.)

## Context

- Lateral thinking aims to restructure patterns of ideas, not to confirm them. It complements vertical (logical) thinking: lateral generates, vertical selects and develops.
- It is probabilistic: it does not guarantee a solution, it raises the chance of finding a better one.
- It applies to every field (design, advertising, science, organization, personal life), not only technical invention.

## Three kinds of stimulus

1. Knowing the principles of lateral thinking and why they are needed.
2. Techniques that act on the problem from the inside.
3. Circumstances that stimulate restructuring, such as an external random stimulus.

## Two ways of exposing yourself to chance

**Exposure:** accept information unrelated to the problem, listen to other people's ideas (even ones you disagree with), talk to people from other fields, walk through a place full of varied objects (a toy shop, a trade fair). Key point: do not look for anything specific; if nothing catches your attention, do not force it.

**Formal selection:** because attention tends to pick what resembles the problem, use a procedure that removes the choosing:
1. A dictionary word, choosing a page number and a word number.
2. A book or magazine chosen by position ("the third copy on the top shelf, right to left").
3. A nearby object chosen by rule ("the nearest red object").

Opening the dictionary and looking for a word you like cancels the effect.

## Why it works

- The mind has a limited area of coherent attention: two inputs held together in focus tend to combine, even with no connection between them.
- The foreign element becomes part of the problem's context and gives it a new direction.
- Mechanisms: restructuring of the pattern; a new entry point; analogy (the word has its own line of thought).
- The less in common with the problem, the more effective it tends to be.

## Practice rules

- **Time:** about 3 minutes, 5 at most. If the flow continues, note the ideas but do not extend the session.
- **One word per occasion:** do not draw another straight away to see if it "fits better". Doing so builds the habit of hunting for the word that confirms the current view.
- **Habit:** practice 3 minutes a day.
- **Confidence:** the most important factor is a calm confidence that something will turn up; at first ideas come hard, and confidence grows with practice.
- **Fixed focus:** the problem is written down before seeing the word (in the exercises a copy is handed to the teacher) so nobody adapts it to the word.

## How the word is used

- It is rarely applied directly: you build a **chain of stepping stones** that starts at the word and reaches the problem.
- Sometimes you **transfer characteristics** of the word to the problem.
- It can work through **wordplay** or through its **opposite**.
- There is no single correct way to use it. The word is only a stimulus.
- The link to the word may become visible only after the idea exists.
- Ideas may be useless or useful; any of them could also be reached by logic, but the stimulus yields many in very little time.

## PO (chapter 20)

PO is a particle signalling that what follows is a provocation or stimulus, not a statement. Applied to the random word it amounts to saying "this word has no relation to the problem; we use it only to see what ideas it produces". You can write `PO <word>`. It also serves to juxtapose two unrelated concepts (`A PO B`), to make disconnected jumps, to use an error as a starting point, and to suspend judgment on an idea.

## Exercises the book proposes

1. Problem + dictionary word (or a short list of 20), 5 to 10 minutes.
2. Same problem with a different word per person; then compare common approaches.
3. Same word with different problems.
4. Participants' own real problems.
5. Physical objects instead of words (assigned, not chosen by the teacher).
