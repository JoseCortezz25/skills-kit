How to read a research report

A quiet, focused guide to moving from a claim on the page to a conclusion you can trust.

01 / Orientation

Start with the question

A research report is not a container of facts. It is an answer to a question, produced through a method, under particular conditions.

Before judging the answer, name the question. What is being explained, compared, measured, or predicted? A precise question gives you a boundary: it tells you which conclusions belong to the report and which ones are being smuggled in by the reader.

Do not ask first, “Is this true?” Ask, “What exactly is being claimed, and what would count as evidence for it?”

The next step follows naturally: once the claim is clear, inspect the path used to support it.

02 / Mechanism

Follow the evidence

Evidence is not the same as volume. A long bibliography can still leave the central claim unsupported. Look for the connection between the claim, the observation, and the method that produced the observation.

Three questions to keep beside the page

What was observed? Separate measurements from interpretations. How was it observed? Check the population, sample, instrument, timeframe, and comparison. What does it support? Test whether the conclusion is narrower or broader than the evidence allows.

Reading note
In a finished report, citations should appear immediately after important factual claims and lead to a numbered source list. Citation density should follow claim importance, not decorate every paragraph.

03 / Judgment

Look for the limits

Good research does not eliminate uncertainty. It makes uncertainty visible. A limitation may come from the data, the design, the definition of a term, or the conditions under which the result was obtained.

When two sources disagree, do not rush to choose a winner. First ask whether they studied different populations, used different definitions, measured different outcomes, or operated in different periods. Disagreement often becomes useful once its dimensions are named.

That habit turns a report from an authority to obey into a model to examine.

04 / Application

A practical reading loop

Read once for structure. Read again for claims. On the third pass, trace the strongest claims to their sources and write down the assumptions that connect evidence to conclusion.

Finish by writing a five-sentence explanation in your own words: the question, the method, the main finding, the strongest caveat, and the decision the evidence can—or cannot—justify.

This loop is deliberately small. The point is not to collect more notes. It is to leave with a clearer model of what is known, how it is known, and where the model stops.
